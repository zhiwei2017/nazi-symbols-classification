\chapter{Data and Model Development Methodology}\label{chapter:methodology}

This chapter outlines the methodology adopted for developing and evaluating the proposed Nazi symbol classification
system. It begins with a detailed overview of the dataset construction process, including the sources of imagery and
structuring strategies tailored for both binary and multi-class classification tasks. The chapter then describes the
taxonomy of Nazi symbols included in the dataset, offering historical and visual context for each symbol class. A
summary of the distribution of these symbols is provided in ~\autoref{tab:symbol-distribution}, while the relative
proportions of the binary and multi-class datasets are visualized in ~\autoref{fig:binary-dataset-pie} and
\autoref{fig:multi-class-dataset-distribution}, respectively. Subsequently, the \ac{ML} and \ac{DL}
models used are introduced, followed by the preprocessing pipeline, training procedures, and hardware
configurations. Finally, the evaluation metrics used to assess model performance are presented, forming the
foundation for the experimental results in the subsequent chapter.


\section{Dataset Construction and Symbol Taxonomy}
\label{sec:data-construction}

To develop a reliable image classification system capable of identifying Nazi symbols in diverse visual contexts, a
custom dataset was curated from multiple public sources. This dataset supports both binary and multi-class
classification tasks by incorporating a broad range of Nazi and non-Nazi imagery, annotated and structured with
attention to historical accuracy, visual clarity, and class balance.

\subsection{Data Sources}

The dataset was assembled from two primary sources:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Roboflow~\footnote{\url{https://roboflow.com}}}: Provided labeled datasets containing images of
  known Nazi symbols such as the swastika, \ac{SS} bolts, and Black Sun. These were selected for their relevance,
  annotation quality, and visual variability, forming the positive class.

  \item \textbf{Kaggle~\footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.com}}}: Contributed general-purpose datasets consisting of
  images unrelated to extremism, including scenes from daily life, commercial logos, and various public domains.
  These constituted the negative class, essential for training models to distinguish hate symbols from benign
  visual content.
\end{itemize}

All datasets were obtained under open-use or research-oriented licenses, with careful review of usage terms and
ethical considerations.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Quality Control}

To ensure dataset reliability and suitability for training, the following preprocessing steps were applied:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Manual Verification}: All symbol annotations were manually reviewed to verify label correctness and
  remove noise.
  \item \textbf{Symbol Taxonomy Alignment}: Nazi symbols were categorized based on visual features and historical
  context, referencing established taxonomies from the \ac{ADL}~\parencite{ADL1913} and academic literature on
  extremist symbology.
  \item \textbf{Deduplication}: Duplicate and near-duplicate images were removed to reduce overfitting and prevent
  data leakage.
  \item \textbf{Partitioning}: The dataset was randomly split into 70\% training, 15\% validation, and 15\% test
  sets, ensuring no overlap of symbol instances across splits.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Symbol Taxonomy and Description}
\label{sec:symbol-taxonomy}

The collected dataset includes 22 distinct symbols associated with Nazi ideology, grouped based on their historical
origin, contemporary usage, and visual form. These symbols were selected due to their recognized use in Nazi Germany
and/or modern neo-Nazi and white supremacist movements. ~\autoref{figure:nazi-symbol-examples} provides a visual
overview.

The following list is a concise description of each symbol class included in the dataset:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nazi Salute}: The Sieg Heil gesture used to demonstrate allegiance to Hitler; currently banned in
  several countries.
  \item \textbf{Flash and Circle}: A lightning bolt within a circle, originally used by the British Union of
  Fascists; later adopted by various white supremacist groups.
  \item \textbf{\ac{SA} Emblem}: Emblem of the Nazi Party's original paramilitary wing, representing
  early violent enforcement of Nazi ideology.
  \item \textbf{Combat 18 Emblem}: Insignia of a violent neo-Nazi group, with “18” representing Adolf Hitler by
  numerical substitution.
  \item \textbf{\ac{SS} Skull (Totenkopf)}: A skull symbol signifying loyalty unto death, worn by \ac{SS} personnel,
  especially in concentration camps.
  \item \textbf{Black Sun}: A circular symbol composed of twelve radial Sig runes, associated with \ac{SS}
  mysticism and adopted by neo-Nazi groups as an esoteric representation of Aryan heritage.
  \item \textbf{Hitler}: Images of Adolf Hitler, representing the central figure of Nazi ideology.
  \item \textbf{Yellow Badge}: A yellow Star of David used to identify Jews during the Holocaust, symbolizing
  systemic persecution.
  \item \textbf{Golden Dawn}: Emblem of a Greek far-right political party with known neo-Nazi affiliations and
  violent activity.
  \item \textbf{Wolfsangel}: Originally a medieval glyph, later adopted by Nazi divisions and modern neo-Nazi movements.
  \item \textbf{Celtic Cross}: While historically religious, its simplified form is now a common white nationalist
  symbol.
  \item \textbf{Broken Sun Cross}: A variation of the ancient sun cross with swastika-like arms; historically linked
  to Nazi-affiliated movements and Waffen-SS units.
  \item \textbf{Hammerskins Emblem}: Two crossed hammers representing a white supremacist skinhead group involved in
  hate music and propaganda.
  \item \textbf{\ac{NOP}}: Symbol of a far-right Polish nationalist group promoting xenophobic and anti-Semitic
  ideologies.
  \item \textbf{Happy Merchant}: A racist caricature of a Jewish man, originating from hate propaganda and widely
  circulated in white supremacist memes.
  \item \textbf{Kolovrat}: An ancient Slavic sun symbol re-appropriated by white supremacist groups in Eastern Europe.
  \item \textbf{Popular Front Emblem}: Used by fascist-aligned organizations; typically combines nationalist imagery
  with militaristic symbols.
  \item \textbf{Identitarian Movement Emblem}: A yellow circle with a black Greek lambda, symbolizing European
  ethno-nationalism and anti-immigration rhetoric.
  \item \textbf{Swastika}: A symbol appropriated by the Nazis to signify racial purity and nationalism; now the most
  recognizable icon of hate worldwide.
  \item \textbf{Atomwaffen Division Emblem}: A stylized nuclear symbol used by a neo-Nazi terrorist group advocating
  for white supremacist accelerationism.
  \item \textbf{Siegrune}: An \ac{SS} lightning-bolt rune adapted from Germanic runes, widely used by the Nazi
  Schutzstaffel.
  \item \textbf{Blood and Honour Emblem}: Derived from the Hitler Youth motto “Blut und Ehre”; used by an
  international neo-Nazi network promoting white nationalist music.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Task-Oriented Dataset Structuring}

To simulate open-set conditions and account for class imbalance, the dataset was structured with deliberate design
choices. Several Nazi-related categories with limited samples were grouped into a new composite class labeled
\textit{neo-Nazi}, allowing the model to generalize over symbol variants while mitigating data sparsity.
Additionally, for the binary classification task, \textit{non-Nazi} images were randomly split into training,
validation, and test sets. As a result, many \textit{non-Nazi} samples in the validation and test sets had no visual or
semantic overlap with training examples. This setup approximates real-world open-set scenarios, where models must
detect unfamiliar or previously unseen content.


The dataset was structured to serve two complementary classification tasks as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Binary Classification (Symbol Detection):} All images containing any of the 22 Nazi-related symbols
  were labeled as \textit{Nazi}, while the remaining images were labeled \textit{non-Nazi}. The dataset includes
  approximately 2,300 positive and 99,000 negative images, resulting in a class ratio of approximately 1:42.

  \item \textbf{Multi-Class Classification (Symbol Identification):} Only images from the 22 Nazi symbol categories
  were included. Each retained its class-specific label, totaling around 2,300 images. Due to severe class
  imbalance, symbol classes with very few samples were grouped into a new composite category labeled
  \textit{neo-Nazi} (see ~\autoref{tab:symbol-distribution}), improving balance and model generalizability
  without sacrificing interpretability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Classification Models}
To rigorously assess the efficacy of various \ac{ML} methodologies in the detection and classification of Nazi
symbols, experiments using a diverse set of models were conducted. This collection comprises both \ac{SotA}
\ac{DL} architectures and traditional \ac{ML} algorithms, enabling a comprehensive comparison across different
computational paradigms and levels of model complexity.

\subsection{Real-Time Object Detection (You Only Look Once: YOLO)}

\ac{YOLO} is a real-time object detection framework that formulates detection as a unified regression problem,
simultaneously predicting bounding boxes and class probabilities directly from full images. By processing the entire
image in a single forward pass, \ac{YOLO} achieves a favorable trade-off between inference speed and accuracy,
rendering it highly suitable for scenarios demanding real-time processing. Although primarily designed for object
detection, \ac{YOLO} can be adapted for classification tasks by assigning the class label corresponding to the
highest-confidence detection. In this study, the pretrained \texttt{YOLOv11-cls} classification model was utilized,
which benefits from improved backbone architectures and refined label assignment strategies that enhance both
robustness and computational efficiency.

\subsection{Residual Network (ResNet)}

\ac{ResNet} is a \ac{CNN} architecture that introduces residual learning through identity
shortcut connections, thereby mitigating the vanishing gradient problem often encountered in training deep networks
~\parencite{he2015deepresiduallearningimage}. This design facilitates the effective training of substantially deep
architectures. The \ac{ResNet} family has consistently demonstrated superior performance in image classification
benchmarks. There was specifically employed \texttt{ResNet34}, which offers a well-established balance between
depth, computational cost, and classification accuracy.

The \texttt{ResNet34} model used in this study is pretrained on the ImageNet dataset, enabling effective transfer
learning for the target classification tasks.

\subsection{EfficientNet}

EfficientNet represents a family of \ac{CNN}s that leverage a compound scaling method to
uniformly scale network depth, width, and resolution. This principled approach yields models that achieve
\ac{SotA} accuracy with significantly fewer parameters and computational resources. Due to its efficiency,
EfficientNet is particularly well-suited for large-scale image classification tasks under constrained hardware
conditions. In this thesis, the \texttt{EfficientNet-B2} variant was employed, which offers a favorable trade-off between
model complexity and accuracy. It serves as a lightweight yet competitive alternative to deeper, more
computationally intensive networks in our experiments. The \texttt{EfficientNet-B2} model used is pretrained on the
ImageNet dataset, enabling transfer learning to improve performance on the target task.

\subsection{Vision Transformer (ViT)}

The \ac{ViT} model innovatively adapts the transformer architecture, originally devised for \ac{NLP}, to vision
tasks by segmenting images into fixed-size patches, which are then treated as a sequence of tokens. It avoids
traditional convolutional operations entirely, instead relying on self-attention mechanisms to model long-range
dependencies and capture global contextual information. \ac{ViT} has demonstrated performance on par with or
exceeding that of conventional \acp{CNN}, particularly when trained on large datasets.

This study employs the pretrained \texttt{vit\_base\_patch16\_224} model to evaluate the effectiveness of
transformer-based architectures in classifying visual symbols that exhibit complex semantic patterns.

\subsection{OpenCLIP}
OpenCLIP is an open-source reimplementation of \ac{CLIP}, a multimodal vision-language model trained on extensive
datasets of image-text pairs. By learning a shared embedding space for both modalities, OpenCLIP enables zero-shot
classification through similarity comparison between image embeddings and text prompt embeddings. In this study,
OpenCLIP is employed in two distinct modes: zero-shot inference and as a fixed feature extractor, providing a
flexible and semantically enriched representation aligned with natural language descriptions.

\subsection{Moondream}
Moondream is a compact multimodal model optimized for low-resource vision-language tasks. Although not explicitly
designed for image classification, it can be adapted for lightweight classification via prompt-based image-text
similarity comparisons. Its strength lies in efficient on-device inference, making it particularly advantageous for
deployment in computationally constrained environments such as mobile or embedded systems. Moondream was investigated
as a potential candidate for these resource-limited classification scenarios.

\subsection{OpenCLIP as Encoder with Classical \ac{ML} Classifiers}
To harness the semantic richness of OpenCLIP embeddings,  OpenCLIP was employed as a frozen feature encoder, generating
fixed-length vector representations for input images. These embeddings subsequently serve as input to a variety of
traditional \ac{ML} classifiers. This two-stage pipeline facilitates rapid experimentation with interpretable,
computationally efficient models, especially beneficial for small datasets or when \ac{DL} approaches incur
excessive computational costs. A detailed summary of the key characteristics of the classification algorithms
employed is presented in ~\autoref{tab:summary-classification-algorithms}.

\paragraph{Classic Classifiers Used:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\ac{LR}:} A linear probabilistic model well-suited for binary classification tasks.
  \item \textbf{\ac{SGD} Classifier:} An efficient linear classifier trained using stochastic gradient descent,
  scalable to large datasets.
  \item \textbf{KNeighbors Classifier:} A classifier implementation of \ac{KNN}, which is a non-parametric,
  instance-based learning algorithm that assigns labels based on the proximity of training examples in feature space.
  \item \textbf{\ac{SVC}:} A margin-based classifier effective in high-dimensional feature spaces, leveraging kernel
  methods to handle non-linear separability.
  \item \textbf{Decision Tree Classifier:} A hierarchical model that partitions the feature space via
  threshold-based splits, enabling interpretable decision rules.
  \item \textbf{Bagging Classifier:} An ensemble approach that aggregates multiple decision trees trained on
  bootstrapped samples to reduce variance and improve stability.
  \item \textbf{Ada Boost Classifier:} A sequential ensemble method that iteratively focuses on misclassified samples
  to enhance overall predictive accuracy.
  \item \textbf{Gradient Boosting Classifier:} Constructs strong predictive models through stage-wise optimization
  of a differentiable loss function using weak learners.
  \item \textbf{Random Forest Classifier:} An ensemble of decision trees trained with random feature selection,
  providing robustness against overfitting and improved generalization.
  \item \textbf{Voting Classifier:} Combines the predictions of multiple base models (e.g., \ac{LR},
  Random Forest, \ac{SVC}) using soft or hard voting strategies to enhance classification performance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data Preprocessing}

To ensure the reliability and consistency of model training, a comprehensive data preprocessing
pipeline comprising resizing, normalization, and offline data augmentation was implemented. These preprocessing
steps are critical to mitigating noise, simulating real-world variations, and facilitating efficient model convergence.

\input{figures/image_size_distribution_pie}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Resizing:} All images were resized to a uniform resolution of $224 \times 224$ pixels. This choice
  was driven by two main factors. First, as illustrated in ~\autoref{fig:image-size-distribution}, analysis
  of the dataset revealed that approximately $71\%$ of Nazi symbol images possess native dimensions smaller than
  $200 \times 200$ pixels, while only $5.5\%$ falls within the $200 \times 200$ to $400 \times 400$ range.
  Standardizing to $224 \times 224$ pixels minimizes distortion and loss of detail for the majority of images. Second,
  several evaluated architectures, including \texttt{vit\_base\_patch16\_224}, require fixed input sizes due to
  their structural constraints. Adopting this resolution facilitates compatibility across models and ensures
  consistent comparative evaluation.

  \item \textbf{Normalization:} Pixel intensities were scaled to the range $[0,1]$ on a per-image basis. This
  normalization stabilizes the optimization process and expedites convergence by ensuring consistent data scaling.

  \item \textbf{Data Augmentation:} Offline data augmentation was employed to artificially enhance the diversity of
  the training set and improve model generalization. Augmentations were applied probabilistically to each sample to
  simulate realistic variations encountered in operational contexts. The augmentation techniques included:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Conversion to grayscale, reducing reliance on color information.
    \item Automatic contrast adjustment, normalizing image dynamic range.
    \item Random horizontal and vertical flipping with a 50\% probability, simulating orientation variability.
    \item Random rotations within $\pm$15°, introducing viewpoint variability.
    \item Random shearing (vertically and horizontally within $\pm$10°), simulating perspective distortions.
    \item Random adjustments to hue ($\pm15$°), saturation ($\pm$63 units), and brightness ($\pm$38 units), increasing
    robustness to lighting and color shifts.
    \item Random exposure modifications to simulate overexposure and underexposure conditions.
    \item Gaussian blur applied randomly to emulate realistic image imperfections.
    \item Sparse addition of random noise (0.1\% probability) to enhance resilience against sensor noise and degradation.
  \end{itemize}
  These augmentations reflect common distortions prevalent in real-world imagery, such as variable lighting, camera
  movement, compression artifacts, and historical degradation, which are particularly pertinent for Nazi symbol
  detection tasks.
\end{itemize}

All preprocessing operations were implemented using a combination of \texttt{Pillow} and \texttt{OpenCV} libraries
during an offline stage. The processing pipeline applies resizing first, followed by augmentation and normalization,
thereby ensuring computational efficiency and uniformity across all samples.

\section{Model Training}

Each model employed in this study was trained with hyperparameter configurations carefully tailored to its
architecture and the specific classification task at hand. To mitigate overfitting, early stopping was implemented
for all \ac{DL} models, monitoring validation loss with a patience of 10 epochs. The model checkpoint achieving the
best validation performance was preserved for subsequent evaluation. A summary of the primary training
hyperparameters is provided in Table~\ref{tab:training-hyperparameters}.

\paragraph{\ac{ResNet}} was trained for 100 epochs using a batch size of 48 and an input resolution of
$224 \times 224$ pixels. The binary classification tasks employed the \ac{BCE} loss, whereas multi-class problems
utilized the \ac{CCE} loss function. Optimization was performed using the Adam algorithm with an initial learning
rate of $1 \times 10^{-3}$ and weight decay set to $1 \times 10^{-5}$. A 1-cycle learning rate scheduler was adopted
to dynamically adjust the learning rate throughout training.

\paragraph{EfficientNet} training spanned 100 epochs with a batch size of 48 and input size $224 \times 224$. For
binary classification, the \ac{NAdam} optimizer was used with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-3}$, no weight
decay, and momentum decay of $4 \times 10^{-3}$. For multi-class tasks, Adam optimizer was applied with a learning rate of
$1 \times 10^{-3}$. The model’s fully connected classification head was replaced with a linear layer matching the
number of target classes.

\paragraph{\ac{ViT}} was trained for 100 epochs with batch size 48 and input resolution $224 \times 224$. The Adam
optimizer with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ was utilized. Loss functions were selected based on the task:
\ac{BCE} for binary classification and \ac{CCE} for multi-class scenarios.

\paragraph{\ac{YOLO}} underwent training for 100 epochs, batch size 48, and input size $224 \times 224$. The
\ac{SGD} optimizer was employed with an initial learning rate of $0.01$, momentum $0.9$, and weight decay
$5 \times 10^{-4}$. Correspondingly, \ac{BCE} and \ac{CCE} losses were applied for binary and multi-class
classification, respectively.

\paragraph{OpenCLIP-based models} leveraged OpenCLIP as a fixed feature extractor to obtain embeddings from resized
input images ($224 \times 224$). Subsequently, classical \ac{ML} classifiers such as \ac{LR}, \ac{SVC},
and KNeighborsClassifier were trained on these embeddings using CPU resources.

Zero-shot classification approaches
with OpenCLIP and Moondream circumvented dataset-specific training entirely; classification was performed by
computing cosine similarity between image and text embeddings, enabling direct inference without fine-tuning.

All training procedures were executed on an NVIDIA RTX 4060Ti GPU with 16 GB of VRAM.

Learning rates were not unified across all models. Instead, they were selected based on architecture-specific best
practices and empirical tuning. Transformer-based models such as \ac{ViT} are sensitive to large gradient steps and
thus require lower learning rates for stable convergence. Conversely, convolutional architectures like \ac{ResNet} and
EfficientNet can tolerate moderately higher learning rates. The values chosen reflect typical recommendations from
the literature and were validated to ensure consistent training dynamics.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:evaluation-metrics}

To quantitatively evaluate classification performance, several standard metrics commonly employed in image
classification tasks were utilized. These metrics offer a multifaceted understanding of model predictive
capabilities for both binary and multi-class problems.

Let the following definitions hold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $TP$: True Positives
    \item $TN$: True Negatives
    \item $FP$: False Positives
    \item $FN$: False Negatives
\end{itemize}

The metrics are formally defined as:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy}:
  \[
  \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
  \]
  Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified instances among all predictions and is most informative
  when class distributions are balanced.

  \item \textbf{Precision}:
  \[
  \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
  \]
  Precision quantifies the model's ability to avoid false positive errors, which is critical when the cost of false
  alarms is high.

  \item \textbf{Recall}:
  \[
  \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
  \]
  Recall assesses the model’s effectiveness in identifying all relevant positive cases, particularly important in
  scenarios where misclassification of positive instances carry severe consequences.

  \item \textbf{F1-Score}:
  \[
  \text{F1-Score} = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
  \]
  The F1-Score harmonizes precision and recall into a single metric, providing a balanced measure suitable for
  imbalanced class distributions.

  \item \textbf{\ac{AUC-ROC} and \ac{AP} (Optional for Binary Classification)}: These are
  threshold-independent metrics computed only for models supporting probabilistic predictions.
  \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{\ac{AUC-ROC}} evaluates a model’s discriminative ability by plotting the \ac{TPR} against the \ac{FPR} across various thresholds:
      \[
      \text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}\text{,} \quad \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN}
      \]
      \item \textbf{\ac{AP}} summarizes the precision-recall curve into a single scalar score by
      averaging precision across all recall levels.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

These metrics were only reported for models that support probabilistic predictions. Classifiers such as
\texttt{\ac{SVC}}, \texttt{SGDClassifier}, and the \texttt{VotingClassifier}, as well as models like
\texttt{Moondream}, were excluded from \ac{AUC-ROC} and \ac{AP} evaluations due to their lack of calibrated
probability outputs. Similarly, the \texttt{OpenCLIP} model was excluded because it operates using cosine similarity
in a high-dimensional embedding space and does not generate confidence scores or probabilistic predictions. As such,
threshold-based metrics like \ac{AUC-ROC} and \ac{AP} are not applicable to its zero-shot, similarity-driven inference
mechanism, and including them would not yield meaningful or interpretable results.
\vspace{1em}

In the \textbf{multi-class setting}, \ac{AUC-ROC} and \ac{AP} were not employed due to their increased complexity and
reduced interpretability when generalized using one-vs-rest or one-vs-one strategies. While macro- and
micro-averaged variants exist, they often obscure class-specific behavior and are less suitable for this study's
imbalanced, symbol-specific dataset. Therefore, macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score were adopted instead,
ensuring equal contribution from all classes.

All evaluation metrics were reported per model under both binary and multi-class settings. For binary
classification, accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and—where applicable—\ac{AUC-ROC} and \ac{AP} were reported. For
multi-class classification, macro-averaged metrics were prioritized. Model selection was guided by validation loss,
and final performance metrics were computed on held-out test sets.
