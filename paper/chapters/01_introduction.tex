\chapter{Introduction}\label{chapter:introduction}
\section{Background}

In recent years, the internet—and particularly social media platforms—has become a key channel for the dissemination
of extremist ideologies and hate-based content. This digital shift has transformed the landscape of radicalization,
allowing fringe groups to amplify their messaging, coordinate activities, and propagate visual symbols of hate with
unprecedented speed and reach. Among the most persistent and globally recognizable of these are Nazi symbols, which
have been adopted and modified by neo-Nazi and far-right movements across different regions and time periods.

Unlike textual hate speech, which has been the subject of extensive moderation and analysis, harmful visual content
remains comparatively under-researched and under-moderated. Symbols such as the swastika, SS bolts, Black Sun, and
stylized versions of Adolf Hitler’s image are often embedded in memes, graffiti, protest photography, or propaganda
posters—frequently in subtle, stylized, or degraded forms that evade both human and machine scrutiny. This creates a
serious challenge for researchers, journalists, and civil society actors seeking to study or counteract online
extremism.

From a technical standpoint, the classification of visual hate symbols represents a complex computer vision task. It
lies at the intersection of iconographic recognition, fine-grained image classification, and open-set detection. The
problem is further compounded by several practical constraints: the scarcity of annotated data, the subtle variation
across symbol styles and contexts, and the risk of algorithmic bias or misuse in real-world deployments.

These challenges are not merely academic. Countries like Germany, Austria, and France have legal restrictions on
Nazi imagery, and international organizations working in counter-extremism need scalable tools for monitoring hate
symbols in open-source imagery. Despite this demand, there is a lack of open, well-documented, and computationally
efficient systems capable of identifying these symbols across varied image sources and visual conditions. This gap
motivates the development of a focused, ethically responsible classification framework—precisely the objective of
this thesis.

\section{Problem Statement}

The automatic detection and classification of Nazi symbols in images is critical for researchers, journalists, civil
society organizations, and online platforms working to monitor and mitigate the spread of extremist content. While
commercial image classification services exist, they are often proprietary, lack transparency, and entail high usage
costs—particularly for applications requiring large-scale image analysis or custom symbol detection. This presents a
significant barrier not for governments per se, but for non-state actors and research groups who often operate under
tight computational and financial constraints.

Even when national institutions have greater resources, scalability and cost-efficiency remain key considerations.
High-throughput image classification tasks, such as analyzing large volumes of online content or social media images
in near real-time, can demand substantial compute time and infrastructure. In practice, this limits the feasibility
of using closed, black-box services that are either too slow, too expensive to scale, or unsuitable for
customization. Moreover, reliance on opaque systems may hinder accountability and reproducibility—both essential in
research and regulatory contexts.

To address these challenges, there is a need for an open, transparent, and efficient image classification system
tailored specifically for Nazi symbol detection. Such a system should support both binary classification: detecting
presence/absence of Nazi imagery, and multi-class classification: identifying specific symbols, while remaining
lightweight and adaptable enough to run on accessible hardware like single-GPU workstations. The same requirements
apply across adjacent domains, including online extremism tracking, digital forensics, and platform content
moderation—making the solution broadly relevant beyond its immediate scope.

\section{Objectives}

This research aims to design, implement, and evaluate an efficient and robust image classification framework for the
automatic detection and categorization of Nazi symbols in visual media. While the immediate focus is on Nazi
iconography, the broader goal is to develop a generalizable and extensible approach that can be adapted to other
categories of extremist or harmful visual symbols. This makes the framework applicable across domains such as civil
society monitoring, journalism, and content moderation, where flexibility, interpretability, and scalability are
critical.

The specific objectives of this study are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dataset Construction:} Assemble a curated dataset of approximately 100,000 images, incorporating both
Nazi-related and non-Nazi imagery to support supervised training, validation, and evaluation.

\item \textbf{Annotation Strategy:} Structure and annotate the dataset to support both binary (presence/absence of
Nazi symbols) and multi-class (identification of specific symbol classes) classification tasks.

\item \textbf{Model Benchmarking:} Implement and evaluate a diverse set of models for visual classification,
including\ac{CNN}s (e.g., ResNet, EfficientNet), transformer-based architectures (e.g., \ac{ViT}),
and multimodal or zero-shot models (e.g., OpenCLIP, Moondream). Additionally, incorporate object
detection-based approaches using \ac{YOLO} to assess region-specific classification performance. Explore both
end-to-end training and hybrid pipelines that combine pretrained visual embeddings with classical machine learning
classifiers.

\item \textbf{Performance Evaluation:} Assess model performance using standard classification metrics (e.g.,
accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and \ac{AUC}), and conduct comparative analyses to identify strengths and
limitations across model types.

\item \textbf{Deployment Readiness:} Develop a lightweight and modular API service that enables efficient model
inference via HTTP requests, facilitating integration into practical research and moderation workflows. The service
is optimized for deployment on resource-constrained environments, such as single-GPU workstations.
\end{itemize}

Despite constraints in data coverage and hardware capacity, the resulting system is designed to be scalable,
modular, and adaptable—capable of supporting future extensions to new symbol categories or broader content
moderation objectives through retraining or fine-tuning.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

The visual symbols associated with Nazi and neo-Nazi ideologies are diverse, evolving, and context-dependent.
Constructing a comprehensive dataset to cover all such variations is inherently challenging. This study therefore
focuses on a representative subset of the most visually distinctive and widely recognized symbols. Specifically, the
classification framework supports the following categories: \textit{Black Sun}, \textit{Flash and Circle},
\textit{Broken Sun Cross}, \textit{The Happy Merchant}, \textit{Hitler}, \textit{Nazi Salute},
\textit{Atomwaffen Division Emblem}, \textit{Blood \& Honor Emblem}, \textit{Celtic Cross},
\textit{Combat 18 Emblem}, \textit{Golden Dawn}, \textit{Hammerskins Emblem},
\textit{Identitarian Movement Emblem}, \textit{Kolovrat}, \textit{National Rebirth of Poland Emblem},
\textit{Popular Front Emblem}, \textit{Yellow Badge}, \textit{Siegrune}, \textit{\ac{SS} Skull},
\textit{Sturmabteilung Emblem}, \textit{Swastika}, and \textit{Wolfsangel}.

Two classification tasks are supported:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Binary Classification:} Detecting the presence or absence of any Nazi-related symbol in a given image.
\item \textbf{Multi-Class Classification:} Identifying the specific symbol (among 13 target classes) when Nazi
imagery is detected.
\end{enumerate}

In addition to dataset limitations, there were also hardware constraints. All experiments were conducted on a single
consumer-grade GPU (NVIDIA RTX 4060Ti, 16GB VRAM). This limited the ability to fine-tune large
models or run memory-intensive experiments—particularly with transformer-based architectures (e.g., \ac{ViT}) or
multimodal embeddings (e.g., OpenCLIP). These models were used in this study, but only in lightweight or partially
frozen configurations to stay within the available hardware limits. It also restricted the feasibility
of training multiple models in parallel for ensembling. As a result, priority was given to computationally efficient
architectures and lightweight training procedures that could fit within the available memory and runtime budget.

Despite these limitations, the system is designed to be modular and scalable. By updating the dataset and retraining
or fine-tuning models, the framework can be extended to other extremist symbol categories or adapted to different
contexts. The emphasis on transparency, efficiency, and accessibility makes the approach particularly suitable for
researchers, journalists, and civil society organizations operating with limited infrastructure.


\section{Thesis Structure}

This thesis is organized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\fullref{chapter:literature-review}} – Surveys existing research on Nazi symbol detection and
classification, emphasizing methodological trends, key findings, and identifying research gaps addressed in this work.

\item \textbf{\fullref{chapter:methodology}} – Describes the data collection process, dataset composition,
preprocessing techniques, and the used classification algorithms. It also outlines the model training process,
evaluation metrics, and justifies the chosen methodologies.

\item \textbf{\fullref{chapter:ethical-considerations}} – Discusses the ethical implications of working with
sensitive content, including concerns around data privacy, potential misuse, and broader responsibilities associated
with automated hate symbol detection.

\item \textbf{\fullref{chapter:results}} – Presents the outcomes of the experiments, including both quantitative and
qualitative analyses of model performance for binary and multi-class classification tasks.

\item \textbf{\fullref{chapter:discussion-and-analysis}} – Interprets the results in relation to the research
objectives, compares findings to prior work, and discusses system limitations and potential improvements.

\item \textbf{\fullref{chapter:model-deployment-and-api-service}} – Describes the deployment strategy for the
best-performing models, including the architecture of the \ac{API} service, the \ac{CI/CD} pipeline, and details of
the available endpoints.

\item \textbf{\fullref{chapter:conclusion-and-future-work}} – Summarizes the thesis contributions, reflects on key
insights gained, and outlines directions for future research in the field of Nazi symbol classification.
\end{itemize}
