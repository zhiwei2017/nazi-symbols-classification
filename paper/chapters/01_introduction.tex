\chapter{Introduction}\label{chapter:introduction}

\section{Background}

Social media platforms have become central hubs for the dissemination of vast quantities of multimedia content,
including images, text, and videos. These data streams are increasingly valuable for research in the social
sciences, particularly in domains such as regional political dynamics, extremism tracking, and hate speech analysis.
To facilitate such studies, researchers often rely on web crawlers to collect relevant content. However, raw data
scraped from the internet typically lacks structure and semantic labeling, necessitating extensive preprocessing.
For image data in particular, classification is a critical preprocessing step that transforms raw images into
structured information suitable for quantitative and qualitative analysis.

Among the many types of harmful or sensitive content on social media, Nazi-related imagery remains a particularly
pressing concern. Nazi symbols, most notably the swastika, were once appropriated by the German National Socialist
regime (1933–1945) and have since become enduring emblems of hate and extremism. Other symbols—such as the SS bolts (
representing the Schutzstaffel), the Black Sun (linked to fascist occult ideologies), and the Siegrune—have also
been adopted by modern neo-Nazi movements. These symbols continue to appear in digital media, where they are used to
spread hate, signal affiliation, or incite violence. Many countries ban or restrict the public display of such
imagery, underscoring the need for robust detection tools to enforce policy and moderate content online.

\section{Problem Statement}

In the context of social science research, the ability to automatically detect and classify Nazi symbols in images
is vital for analyzing trends in extremist communication and for enforcing legal or ethical guidelines on hate
speech. However, commercial services offering image classification capabilities—especially for hate symbols—are
often expensive and proprietary. This poses a significant barrier to researchers who wish to conduct large-scale
studies with limited funding.

To address this gap, an affordable, open, and effective classification system is needed. Such a system must be
capable of both detecting the presence of Nazi symbols (binary classification) and identifying the specific symbol
class (multi-class classification). The system must also be adaptable, transparent, and lightweight enough to run on
limited hardware resources, such as a single GPU workstation.

\section{Objectives}

This study aims to design and evaluate a robust and efficient image classification framework for the automatic
detection and categorization of Nazi symbols. Specifically, we:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Construct a curated dataset of approximately 100,000 images containing both Nazi-related and non-Nazi imagery.
  \item Annotate and structure the dataset to support both binary and multi-class classification tasks.
  \item Investigate a range of deep learning and multimodal models—including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs),
  Vision Transformers (ViTs), and OpenCLIP embeddings—paired with classical machine learning algorithms.
  \item Evaluate the models based on standard classification metrics and analyze their strengths and weaknesses.
  \item Develop and package an API service that allows for model inference via HTTP requests, enabling practical
  deployment.
\end{itemize}

While the dataset and scope are constrained, our overarching objective is to provide a generalizable methodology
that can be extended to larger datasets and broader symbol categories.

\section{Scope and Limitations}

Due to the vast number and variation of Nazi and neo-Nazi symbols used historically and in contemporary extremist
contexts, it is impractical to assemble a complete dataset. The dataset used in this study represents a subset of
the most commonly encountered or visually distinctive symbols. Specifically, this study includes the following Nazi
and neo-Nazi symbols: \textit{Sonnenrad}, \textit{Flash and circle}, \textit{Broken Sun Cross},
\textit{The Happy Merchant}, \textit{Hitler}, \textit{Nazi salute}, \textit{Atomwaffen Division emblem},
\textit{Blood \& Honor emblem}, \textit{Celtic Cross}, \textit{Combat 18 emblem}, \textit{Golden Dawn},
\textit{Hammerskins emblem}, \textit{Identitarian movement emblem}, \textit{Kolovrat},
\textit{National Rebirth of Poland emblem}, \textit{Popular front emblem}, \textit{Yellow badge}, \textit{Siegrune},
\textit{SS Skull}, \textit{Sturmabteilung emblem}, \textit{Swastika}, and \textit{Wolfsangel}.

This research addresses two main classification tasks:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Binary classification:} Determine whether a given image contains any Nazi-related symbol.
  \item \textbf{Multi-class classification:} Identify which specific symbol (among the 13 defined categories) is
  present in a Nazi-related image.
\end{enumerate}

In addition to dataset limitations, this study is constrained by hardware availability. All experiments were
conducted using a single GPU with 16 GB of memory. This limitation necessitated the use of resource-efficient models
and restricted the complexity of hyperparameter tuning and model ensembling.

Despite these constraints, this work aims to demonstrate the feasibility of developing effective and efficient
classification tools to assist researchers, content moderators, and civil society stakeholders in identifying
extremist visual content.
