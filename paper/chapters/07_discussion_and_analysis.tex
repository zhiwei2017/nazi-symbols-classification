\chapter{Discussion and Analysis}\label{chapter:discussion-and-analysis}

\subsection{Binary Classification}

The results presented in Table~\autoref{tab:binary-results} highlight substantial variation in performance across
different models. Notably, classical machine learning classifiers using OpenCLIP embeddings achieved some of the
highest scores overall. The \textbf{OpenCLIP + SVC} model delivered the best F1-score (0.939), indicating a strong
balance between precision (0.948) and recall (0.929). Similarly, \textbf{OpenCLIP + KNeighborsClassifier} attained
the highest AUC (0.966) and the best recall (0.936), demonstrating its effectiveness at detecting positive (Nazi)
samples.

Among deep learning models, both \textbf{YOLOv11} and \textbf{ViT} performed competitively, with F1-scores of 0.889
and 0.890, respectively. These results suggest that strong feature extraction capabilities (as seen in ViT) and
object-localization attention (as leveraged by YOLOv11) benefit binary classification, even in a non-detection context.

Interestingly, the \textbf{Moondream} model achieved perfect recall (1.000) but had an extremely low precision (
0.046), leading to a poor F1-score (0.088). This implies a heavy bias toward predicting the Nazi class, resulting in
numerous false positives. The \textbf{OpenCLIP zero-shot model} (without classifier) similarly performed poorly,
emphasizing the value of fine-tuning or classifier integration on task-specific data.

Traditional ensemble classifiers (e.g., GradientBoosting, AdaBoost, VotingClassifier) using OpenCLIP features
generally outperformed standalone CNNs like ResNet and EfficientNet. This reinforces the advantage of combining rich
pretrained vision-language embeddings with well-established decision models in low-data or imbalanced scenarios.

In summary, models integrating OpenCLIP embeddings with classical classifiers clearly outperform others in this
binary task. However, deep learning models like ViT and YOLOv11 still show strong potential, especially when trained
end-to-end with high-resolution inputs.

\subsection{Binary Classification with Reduced Nazi Symbol Classes}

To further examine model robustness and adaptability, we conducted a binary classification experiment restricted to
a reduced subset of Nazi symbols (\textit{siegrune}, \textit{swastika}, and \textit{SS skull}) against the full set
of non-Nazi images. The goal was to evaluate model performance when the semantic diversity of the positive class is
reduced.

Despite the narrower scope, overall trends remained consistent with the full binary task. Transformer-based
architectures continued to perform strongly. ViT achieved excellent results with an F1-score of 0.948 and an AUC of
0.962, indicating its strong ability to generalize even with reduced symbol variety. Among OpenCLIP-based hybrid
models, \textit{OpenCLIP + KNeighborsClassifier} attained the highest AUC of \textbf{0.993}, while
\textit{OpenCLIP + SVC} achieved the best overall performance with an accuracy of \textbf{0.998}, precision of
\textbf{0.973}, recall of \textbf{0.900}, and F1-score of \textbf{0.935}.

Interestingly, YOLOv11 underperformed in this setup (F1-score 0.661, AUC 0.783) compared to its results on the full
binary task, potentially due to its reliance on spatial object features that may be less effective when class
variety is reduced. Conversely, models like Moondream and OpenCLIP hybrids maintained high performance, indicating
that text-image aligned embeddings may still effectively capture distinguishing features in constrained settings.

This experiment highlights that reducing the complexity of the positive class does not necessarily simplify the task
uniformly for all models. While it benefits embedding-based methods and transformers, object detection models like
YOLOv11 may be affected due to loss of visual diversity.

\subsection{Multi-Class Classification}

Table~\autoref{tab:multi-class-results} summarizes the performance of all evaluated models in the multi-class
classification task. The \textbf{YOLOv11} model achieved the best overall performance across all metrics, with a
macro-averaged accuracy of 0.883, precision of 0.842, recall of 0.845, and F1-score of 0.835. This strong
performance is likely attributed to its ability to capture fine-grained features in high-resolution images, coupled
with its efficient architectural design.

The \textbf{ViT} model also performed well, ranking second across all metrics (e.g., F1-score of 0.790),
demonstrating that transformer-based architectures are effective at capturing complex spatial and semantic
relationships in images. In contrast, traditional CNN-based models like \textbf{ResNet} and especially
\textbf{EfficientNet} showed weaker performance. ResNet reached a reasonable accuracy of 0.819 but struggled with
recall (0.655), while EfficientNet severely underperformed (F1-score of only 0.048), suggesting sensitivity to model
input size or optimization instability for multi-class tasks.

The \textbf{OpenCLIP zero-shot model} alone performed poorly, with an accuracy of 0.274 and F1-score of 0.265,
highlighting the limitations of general-purpose vision-language models when applied directly to highly specific
symbol recognition tasks without fine-tuning.

When combining OpenCLIP embeddings with classical machine learning classifiers, performance varied widely. Notably,
\textbf{OpenCLIP + SGDClassifier} and \textbf{OpenCLIP + SVC} stood out, achieving F1-scores of 0.632 and 0.620
respectively, outperforming other classical classifiers. This suggests that linear models and kernel methods can
partially leverage the semantic richness of OpenCLIP features. However, ensemble-based classifiers like
\textbf{RandomForest}, \textbf{Bagging}, and \textbf{GradientBoosting} generally performed worse, indicating
potential overfitting or a mismatch between embedding distributions and classifier assumptions.

Overall, models that operate end-to-end with high-quality image representations, such as YOLOv11 and ViT, clearly
outperform both zero-shot methods and OpenCLIP embedding pipelines in this multi-class setting. This emphasizes the
importance of feature specialization and architectural alignment for symbol classification tasks involving subtle
visual differences across many classes.
