\chapter{Literature Review}\label{chapter:literature-review}

The classification of Nazi symbols in images is a highly specialized task that intersects multiple domains including
computer vision, hate speech detection, few-shot learning, and ethical \ac{AI} deployment. Given the sensitive and
varied visual forms of Nazi symbols, this problem introduces unique challenges such as the need for robust visual
representations, limited training data, and open-set recognition capabilities.

\section{Visual Symbol and Logo Recognition}

Early work in symbol and logo classification has laid the foundation for the visual recognition of hate symbols.
Iandola et al.~\parencite{iandola2015deeplogo} demonstrated that \ac{CNN}s can effectively
recognize logos in natural scenes, which parallels the need for recognizing hate symbols in complex visual contexts.
To support large-scale classification, Wang et al.~\parencite{li2020logo2k} proposed Logo-2K+, a dataset with over 2,000
logo categories, emphasizing the importance of intra-class variation and scalability.

Real-world deployment of Nazi symbol classifiers must handle unseen or altered symbol variants. Tüzkö et al.~\parencite{tuzko2020open}
addressed this issue through open-set logo detection, where systems are trained to identify
both known and novel logos. This is directly applicable to Nazi symbol detection, which must generalize to stylistic
and contextual variations across images.

\section{Hate Content Detection in Social Media and Memes}

The intersection of visual and textual hate content has been studied in the context of online memes and extremist
messaging. Belete and Alitasb~\parencite{belete2025200258} developed a \ac{DL} pipeline for detecting offensive
Amharic-language memes using \ac{BiLSTM} and Dense architectures. Though language-specific, their framework provides
useful insights for multimodal hate symbol classification, where visual symbols are embedded in broader meme contexts.

Das Bhattacharjee et al.~\parencite{dasbhattacharjee2019365} explored the detection of malicious content in social
platforms using a context-aware active learning approach. Their findings highlight the importance of incorporating
contextual features, a principle relevant when Nazi symbols are subtly integrated into broader visual compositions.

Scrivens et al.~\parencite{scrivens2021dangerous} further analyzed how violent right-wing extremists use imagery online.
Their sociological insights underscore the importance of identifying not just overt symbols, but also subtler visual
cues tied to extremist behavior.

\section{\ac{DL} Models for Visual Classification}

The backbone of Nazi symbol classification lies in deep visual models. Krizhevsky et al.~\parencite{krizhevsky2012imagenet}
introduced AlexNet, a pioneering \ac{CNN} architecture that enabled large-scale image
classification. \ac{VGGNet}~\parencite{simonyan2014very} demonstrated the value of deeper networks, which can extract
nuanced visual features—critical when symbols are partially obscured or stylized.

Dosovitskiy et al.~\parencite{dosovitskiy2020image} proposed the \ac{ViT}, which forgoes convolutions in
favor of self-attention, enabling the model to consider global image context. This is especially relevant when Nazi
symbols appear in complex backgrounds or compositions.

\section{Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Symbol Recognition}

Nazi symbol classification often lacks sufficient labeled training examples, particularly for rare or obscure
variations. This makes zero-shot and few-shot learning indispensable. Xian et al.~\parencite{xian2017zeroshot}
systematically evaluated \ac{ZSL} approaches, emphasizing benchmark consistency and generalization.

Radford et al.~\parencite{radford2021clip} introduced \ac{CLIP}, a powerful model that aligns image and text
representations through contrastive learning. Its ability to perform zero-shot classification using only textual
descriptions makes it well-suited for detecting hate symbols that are rare or constantly evolving.
DiffCLIP~\parencite{zhang2021language} expands on this by improving few-shot classification through
prompt-based tuning, a strategy ideal for small Nazi symbol datasets.

Few-shot learning methods like the \ac{RN}~\parencite{sung2018learning} offer another pathway, using
similarity-based reasoning to classify novel instances with only a few examples. This complements \ac{ZSL}
approaches and can be effective when a limited annotated dataset of Nazi symbols is available.

\section{Dataset Transparency and Ethical Considerations}

Given the sensitive nature of Nazi symbol detection, ethical \ac{AI} development is crucial. Gebru et al.
~\parencite{gebru2021datasheets} advocated for “datasheets for datasets” to ensure transparency in dataset creation and
usage—critical when handling hate-related imagery. In parallel, Shahriari~\parencite{ieee2021ethics}
emphasized ethically aligned design principles, stressing the importance of prioritizing human wellbeing and
fairness when deploying \ac{AI} systems in politically sensitive domains.
