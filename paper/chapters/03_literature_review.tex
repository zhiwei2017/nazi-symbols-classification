\chapter{Literature Review}\label{chapter:literature-review}

This chapter reviews foundational and contemporary research relevant to the automated recognition of Nazi symbols in
images. While direct studies on Nazi iconography remain limited due to ethical and legal sensitivities, substantial
insights can be drawn from adjacent domains including visual symbol recognition
~\parencite{krizhevsky2012imagenet,iandola2015deeplogo}, hate content detection in social media
~\parencite{belete2025200258,dasbhattacharjee2019365}, multimodal meme analysis~\parencite{scrivens2021dangerous},
and general-purpose image classification~\parencite{dosovitskiy2020image}.

Particular emphasis is placed on methods for handling low-resource classification tasks, such as few-shot and
zero-shot learning~\parencite{radford2021clip,zhang2021language}, which are highly relevant given the scarcity and
variability of hate symbol imagery. The review also explores techniques for open-set recognition
~\parencite{tuzko2020open}—a critical consideration in this work, where unseen or stylized symbol variants
frequently appear in real-world data.

In addition, the chapter surveys research on dataset design and ethical considerations, including best practices for
data transparency, annotation documentation, and the risks of deploying AI in politically sensitive
contexts~\parencite{gebru2021datasheets,ieee2021ethics}. These studies help frame both the technical direction and
ethical responsibility of this work, underscoring challenges such as visual ambiguity, adversarial content creation,
and the importance of generalization across diverse symbol types.

Collectively, the literature lays a conceptual and methodological foundation for the design decisions made in this
thesis—from model selection and training regimes to dataset structuring and evaluation strategies.

\section{Visual Symbol and Logo Recognition}

Foundational research in logo and symbol recognition has profoundly influenced methods for visual hate symbol
detection. Iandola et al.\parencite{iandola2015deeplogo} demonstrated the effectiveness of convolutional neural
networks (\ac{CNN}s) in recognizing logos within natural scenes—an analogous challenge to detecting Nazi symbols
embedded in visually complex and cluttered environments such as posters, graffiti, or digital memes. Their work
highlights the critical role of convolutional architectures in capturing localized visual patterns essential for
precise symbol identification. Addressing scalability and intra-class variability, Wang et al.
\parencite{li2020logo2k} introduced Logo-2K+, a large-scale dataset encompassing over 2,000 logo categories. This
dataset emphasizes the importance of model generalization to distinguish visually similar but distinct classes, a
challenge directly mirrored in the present work’s need to robustly differentiate various Nazi symbols from benign
but visually related imagery despite limited labeled data. Collectively, these foundational studies establish both
the architectural framework and dataset design principles that inform the adaptation of convolutional models to the
nuanced demands of hate symbol recognition.

Real-world deployment of Nazi symbol recognition systems must also accommodate significant visual
variability—including stylistic modifications, degraded or occluded imagery, and previously unseen symbol variants.
Tüzkö et al.~\parencite{tuzko2020open} tackled these challenges using open-set logo detection techniques that enable
models to distinguish known categories from ambiguous or novel inputs. This open-set capability is crucial in
practice, given that hate symbols frequently appear in altered or contextually obscured forms—whether partially
hidden, stylized, or embedded within complex visual scenes such as memes or graffiti.

Guided by these insights, this study simulates open-set conditions through strategic dataset partitioning:
\textit{non-Nazi} images that are visually unrelated to the training data are included in the test set, while rare
Nazi symbol categories are consolidated into a broader \textit{neo-Nazi} class to promote generalization across
scarce classes. Consequently, model evaluation extends beyond traditional closed-set accuracy metrics to
incorporate robustness assessments under open-set-like conditions, thereby better reflecting real-world challenges
and deployment scenarios.

Recent advances have explored weakly supervised approaches for symbol recognition
~\parencite{tang2017multipleinstancedetectionnetwork}, particularly suited to domains characterized by sparse or
noisy annotations—common in extremist content datasets. Building on this, the current work leverages a pretrained
visual encoder originally trained on large-scale general image datasets, which is then fine-tuned on a carefully
curated hate symbol dataset annotated with domain-specific labels. This transfer learning strategy enables the model
to retain broad visual recognition capabilities while adapting to the challenges of Nazi symbol classification,
including limited data and high intra-class variation. While Tang et al.
~\parencite{tang2017multipleinstancedetectionnetwork} focus on learning symbol localization from coarse image-level
labels via saliency maps, the present approach incorporates high-quality textual prompts and OpenCLIP embeddings to
direct classification toward semantically relevant image regions. This reduces reliance on pixel-precise annotations
without sacrificing discriminative power, aligning with the efficiency goals of weak supervision and avoiding the
need for costly bounding box labels.

\section{Hate Content Detection in Social Media and Memes}

Hate symbol recognition increasingly intersects with multimodal content analysis, especially in the context of memes
and online extremist propaganda. Belete and Alitasb~\parencite{belete2025200258} proposed a deep learning pipeline
for detecting offensive Amharic-language memes by combining \ac{BiLSTM} and dense networks, highlighting the
effectiveness of jointly modeling textual and visual features. While their work is language-specific, it
demonstrates a broader principle highly relevant to this research: Nazi symbols in contemporary media often appear
alongside provocative slogans or textual overlays, necessitating models that can parse both image and text. In
alignment with this, the present work integrates OpenCLIP-based vision-language embeddings, which allow the
classifier to attend to semantically rich regions informed by textual prompts—an essential design choice for parsing
symbolic content within complex meme compositions.

Similarly, Das Bhattacharjee et al.~\parencite{dasbhattacharjee2019365} employed a context-aware active learning
framework to detect malicious online content, emphasizing the importance of contextual information. This is directly
relevant to the current study, where hate symbols may appear in highly ambiguous visual settings—either stylized or
partially obscured. To mirror these real-world complexities, the evaluation strategy in this study includes
scenarios where Nazi-related imagery is embedded in diverse, often noisy backgrounds, challenging the model’s
ability to generalize beyond sanitized training samples.

Furthermore, Scrivens et al.~\parencite{scrivens2021dangerous} analyzed how violent right-wing extremist groups use
images to signal affiliation or ideology, often relying on covert symbolism. Their sociological findings reinforce
the necessity of visual detection approaches that can handle subtle, disguised, or contextually loaded symbols. This
perspective underpins the symbolic granularity of the classification schema in the current work, which goes
beyond surface-level image matching to detect and disambiguate nuanced forms of Nazi symbolism encountered in
user-generated online content.

\section{Deep Learning Models for Visual Classification}

Modern image classification systems largely rely on deep neural networks, which form the backbone of the symbol
recognition pipeline developed in this study. AlexNet~\parencite{krizhevsky2012imagenet} marked a pivotal
advancement in \ac{DL} by introducing a convolutional architecture capable of large-scale image classification. This
laid the groundwork for subsequent developments in visual pattern recognition, which are essential when detecting
visual hate symbols embedded in diverse and noisy real-world imagery.

VGGNet~\parencite{simonyan2014very} further demonstrated that deeper convolutional networks can effectively capture
fine-grained visual details. This capability is particularly relevant to the challenges addressed here, as many Nazi
symbols are small, partially obscured, or appear in degraded conditions—e.g., embedded within low-resolution memes
or worn-out graffiti. While VGGNet itself is not directly used in this work, the core principle it highlights—that
deep \ac{CNN}s can learn hierarchical visual features—remains essential for symbol localization and class
differentiation. This concept is reflected in the convolutional backbones adopted in this research, such as ResNet
and EfficientNet, which build on the same representational strengths to support robust classification under such
constraints.

More recently, \ac{ViT}~\parencite{dosovitskiy2020image} have introduced a shift from localized
convolutional operations to global self-attention mechanisms. This architectural innovation allows models to
consider the entire image context when making predictions—an important advantage when Nazi symbols are entangled
with background clutter or positioned alongside provocative text. In this study, ViT serves as one of the baseline
architectures to benchmark performance in such visually complex scenarios, complementing convolution-based models
and offering comparative insights into context-aware symbol classification.

By evaluating both \ac{CNN}-based and Transformer-based models, this research identifies the architectures most
effective
at handling the variability, subtlety, and complexity inherent in extremist visual content.

\section{Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Symbol Recognition}

A persistent challenge in Nazi symbol classification lies in the scarcity of labeled training data, particularly for
rare, emerging, or region-specific variations. These limitations make it difficult to apply fully supervised
methods, motivating the adoption of zero-shot and few-shot learning strategies to support generalization in
low-resource settings.

Xian et al.~\parencite{xian2017zeroshot} conducted a comprehensive benchmark of \ac{ZSL} approaches, emphasizing the
trade-offs between generalization and domain adaptation—an issue especially relevant when trying to recognize
visually diverse hate symbols with limited examples. Their findings inform the current approach, which relies on
pretrained vision-language models to compensate for sparse supervision.

CLIP, introduced by Radford et al.\parencite{radford2021clip}, aligns image and text representations through
contrastive learning\parencite{chen2020simpleframeworkcontrastivelearning}, enabling classifiers to associate
symbolic imagery with descriptive prompts even without class-specific training. This capability is leveraged via
OpenCLIP embeddings, allowing Nazi-related symbols to be recognized based on semantic cues such as “swastika” or “SS
emblem,” thus facilitating zero-shot classification for underrepresented or novel categories.

DiffCLIP~\parencite{zhang2021language} extends this paradigm by enabling prompt tuning for few-shot scenarios.
Building on this idea, the research employs carefully engineered prompts and domain-curated symbol descriptions to
adapt the pretrained model to hate symbol classification without requiring extensive per-class supervision. This is
particularly beneficial when dealing with minority classes, such as lesser-known neo-Nazi symbols, or images
exhibiting stylistic alterations.

\ac{RN}~\parencite{sung2018learning} offer another complementary perspective by classifying unseen
categories based on similarity in feature space. While RNs are not directly implemented, the underlying idea of
distance-based matching informs the use of classic machine learning models (e.g., k-NN, SVM) operating on
CLIP-derived embeddings. This design choice enables the system to generalize beyond the training
distribution—capturing subtle visual relationships and supporting recognition of stylized or obscured symbols not
encountered during fine-tuning.

Together, these zero- and few-shot learning frameworks provide essential scaffolding for symbol recognition in
settings where labeled data are scarce, guiding the architecture and training strategies used in this research to
address both generalization and semantic alignment.

\section{Dataset Transparency and Ethical Considerations}

The sensitive and potentially harmful nature of Nazi symbol detection demands rigorous attention to transparency,
accountability, and ethical responsibility throughout the machine learning lifecycle. Gebru et al.~
\parencite{gebru2021datasheets} introduced the “datasheets for datasets” framework, advocating for structured
documentation that details dataset provenance, collection methods, annotation processes, and potential biases. This
approach is directly applied in the present research through detailed dataset reporting, including symbol class
distributions, labeling rationale, and preprocessing decisions. By adopting such practices, the project ensures that
downstream users and evaluators can understand the dataset’s limitations, sources, and intended use cases—crucial
when handling politically charged or culturally sensitive content.

In parallel, Shahriari~\parencite{ieee2021ethics} highlights the importance of ethically aligned AI design,
emphasizing principles such as human rights, safety, transparency, and fairness. These considerations directly
inform the system’s development, especially given the risks associated with false positives (e.g., benign religious
or cultural symbols misclassified as hate-related) or unintended surveillance use. In response, the research
includes deliberate measures to reduce harm: symbol definitions are carefully grounded in recognized legal and
sociological classifications, performance metrics are stratified to monitor class-wise precision and recall, and
model interpretability is supported via prompt-based architectures and semantic embeddings.

Together, these ethical frameworks shape not only how the dataset is constructed and documented, but also how the
classifier is evaluated, interpreted, and contextualized. This commitment to transparency and responsible design
aligns the technical contributions with the broader societal impact of deploying hate symbol recognition systems in
practice.
